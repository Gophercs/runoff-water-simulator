      How the water moves

      Each cell of the map holds a water depth. Every step, the flow across each cell edge speeds up or slows down according to the difference in water surface height either side, and is held back by ground roughness (Manning's n). This is the local inertial method used by the LISFLOOD-FP flood model (Bates et al. 2010, with de Almeida et al. 2012's smoothing). The step length shrinks automatically when water gets deep so it stays stable.

      Checks it passes: a pool settles dead flat, water is conserved to rounding error, and rain on a uniform slope reaches the textbook Manning depth to within about 1.5%.

      Soaking-in uses the Green-Ampt method with the Rawls, Brakensiek & Miller (1983) soil parameters: dry soil takes water fast at first, slowing towards a steady rate as it wets. Land cover scales that rate and sets the roughness; those multipliers are rough guides, and woodland in particular varies a lot in the field.

      Not for flood-risk decisions. Runoff is a well-behaved model, not a validated one: treat what it shows as a way of thinking about where water goes, and check anything important against what actually happens on the ground and against official flood maps.

      Known limits: one soil and one land cover for the whole area. Soil is treated as deep, so shallow soils or a high water table filling up (common on UK hillsides in winter) aren't modelled; the "Saturated" start is the stand-in for that. Bridges and culverts are often missing or solid in a DTM, so roads can act as dams. Very shallow sheets of water on steep ground are this method's weakest case.
